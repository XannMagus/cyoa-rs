You wake before the light does, as you always do, with your hand already closed on the rail of the iron stair.

The Pale Light hums in its ribs. Eleven nights of wind have found every loose rivet in her, and you know each one by its note. But the note you listen for is not in the tower. It is a low, cracked, bronze voice that should be rolling across the water from the breakwater, tolling the turn of the tide. For eleven mornings you have woken and waited for it, and for eleven mornings the silence has come in through the gallery door like a damp draught.

You climb to the lamp room and douse the flame. Beyond the glass the sea is the colour of an old pewter plate. Mist lies on it in long, slow sheets. To the north the Old Light is a smudge of whitewash, and between you and it lie the Teeth. They are black and patient, and at this hour the falling tide has bared their spines. Beyond them, at the end of the breakwater, you can just make out the timber frame where the bell hung. It is an empty gallows now, with a cut rope stirring in the wind.

*Low water in an hour. The causeway will be clear.*

You do not tell yourself why you are going ashore. The larder is thin, and that is true enough. But you take your peaked cap from its hook, button the oilskin to the throat, and stand a moment with your thumb on the pale scar through your eyebrow, as you do when you are lying to yourself.

You were in this room that night. The fog was thick as wool, and you saw a shuttered lantern wink from the Drowned Stair: three slow, one quick, a pause, then three slow again. Below it, a low boat slid out across the black water with no oar-beat and no sail, only a faint wet hush. You knew the pattern. You had flashed it yourself once, from a pilot's cutter, in another life and under another name. You wrote nothing in the log. You said nothing to anyone, not even the other Ajax, who nodded to you at the chandler's on the third day with a look that suggested he had seen something too.

The causeway is a spine of slick stone, furred green and sucking at your boots. You walk it with your eyes down and your ears wide open. Gulls quarrel over a stranded crab. Water drains from the weed in a thousand tiny taps. The mist muffles everything, so that the town seems to come toward you in pieces: first the smell of tar and smoke, then a rope creaking against a piling, then the grey stack of slate roofs climbing the hill in their crooked terraces.

At the foot of the quay steps, someone has nailed a fresh notice over the old one. You read it with the stillness of a man reading his own sentence.

**THE WIDOW'S BELL. Return, no questions asked. Harbourmaster's Office.**

Below it, in pencil and in a different, angrier hand, someone has written: *The sea is listening.*

You are about to turn away when you feel it, the old prickle at the back of the neck. You do not look round. You study the notice instead, and let your eyes slide sideways along the wet stone of the quay. A figure is crouched between two lobster pots. She is thin and sandy-haired, in a jumper far too big for her, and she is watching you with sharp green eyes. When she sees you notice her, she does not run. She only goes very still, the way a gull goes still on a rock.

Out past her shoulder, at the end of the breakwater, the sea has drawn back far enough to bare the lowest steps of the Drowned Stair. They are black and weedy, and they lead down to a door that no one admits to using. The door stands slightly ajar.

The tide will not stay out long. The girl is watching you. And somewhere behind the smokehouse a door bangs, and a heavy voice begins to shout about judgement.

You decide the way you have always decided, which is by letting your body move before your reasons catch up. You turn from the notice and walk, not toward the shouting, and not toward the breakwater, but toward the lobster pots.

The girl does not bolt. Her fingers tighten on the slimed rim of a pot, and her eyes flick once to the gap between the smokehouse and the net-racks, measuring the distance. You stop a good eight feet off and lower yourself onto an upturned crate, slowly, the way you would approach a spooked gull on a rail. Your knees complain. You take off your cap and turn it in your hands.

"Larder's thin," you say, to no one in particular. "Thought I'd see if the chandler still sells biscuit that doesn't taste of the sea."

Nothing. A rope creaks. Behind the smokehouse the heavy voice rolls on: *"...a judgement, I say, and you'll not buy your way round it with a notice!"* There is the thin answering murmur of someone trying to reason with it and failing.

"It doesn't," the girl says at last. Her voice is hoarse, a voice that spends its days shouting orders over tavern noise. "Taste of the sea, I mean. Biscuit. It tastes of the chandler's cat." She says it flatly, but one corner of her mouth has betrayed her.

"That's worse," you say gravely.

She studies you. You are used to being studied, and you know the particular look: a person adding up a stranger's rumours. *The Pale Light. The one who came from the south. Doesn't drink at the Eel.* You let her finish the sum.

"You're the far one," she says. "Not the Old Light. The one on the rock."

"Ajax," you say. "Both of us, I'm afraid. It's a trial to everyone."

"I know." She almost smiles again. Then her gaze slides past your shoulder, to the end of the breakwater, and her face closes like a shutter. It is only a moment, but you have spent fifteen years watching doorways, and you see it. She has looked at the Drowned Stair the way you looked at it that night.

"You were watching me before I read the notice," you say quietly. "Before I'd even reached the steps."

"I watch everyone." Her chin comes up. "Maud says it's rude."

"Maud's likely right. And I'm not cross." You wait a breath. "I only wonder what a person watches for, on a morning like this."

The mist shifts. For a moment the whole breakwater stands clear, black and glistening, the empty timber frame at its end like a gallows against the pewter sky. Water is already beginning to whisper back over the lowest rocks. You have perhaps an hour, no more, before the Stair goes under again and takes its door with it.

The girl's knuckles whiten on the pot. "Wheels," she says, so low you nearly lose it beneath the gulls. "That's all. I didn't see nothing. I only *heard*."

Your heart gives one slow, heavy knock.

"Wheels," you repeat, gently.

"And a horse. With its feet wrapped up, like in sacking, so it went all soft." She stops, and her eyes go wide, as though the words had climbed out without her leave. "I never told. I'll be blamed. They always say it's me, whatever goes."

Before you can answer, a boot scrapes on the stones behind the smokehouse, and the shouting breaks off mid-word. A silence follows, the particular silence of a large man who has seen something and is deciding what it means.

Around the corner of the net-racks comes a figure you know by his bulk before his face: white-bearded, shovel-handed, a sealskin waistcoat stretched across a chest like a cask. **Brannoch Teale.** His broken nose points at you like a compass needle, and behind him, half-hidden, two younger men in Guild jerseys stand with their arms folded.

"Well now," he says, and the voice that was thundering a moment ago drops to something worse, something slow and grave. "The Pale Light comes ashore. Eleven days the sea's gone quiet, and the *far* keeper picks today to take the air."

The girl has gone rigid beside you. When you glance down, the gap between the pots is empty, and a sandy head is already vanishing around the corner of the net-racks, bare feet slapping wet stone.

Teale does not look after her. He looks at you, and then, deliberately, past you, toward the Drowned Stair and its open door.

"Tide's turning," he says. "You'll want to be sure of your footing, keeper."

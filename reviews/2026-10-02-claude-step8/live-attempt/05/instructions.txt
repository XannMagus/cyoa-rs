**Merrow Quay** is a small tidal harbour on a grey northern coast, cupped in a horseshoe of black rock and shingle. Fishing skiffs and a few coastal traders crowd its stone quay. Behind it, slate-roofed houses climb the hill in crooked terraces, and a salt marsh, the Wicker Flats, runs inland to a road that few people use. Mist comes in off the water most mornings. It muffles sound and lets the town hear its own small noises: ropes creaking, gulls arguing, the slap of water on the old piles.

*For two hundred years, the harbour has been ruled by a bell.*

The **Widow's Bell**, a bronze bell the height of a child, hung in an open timber frame at the end of the long breakwater. It was cast from the melted fittings of a ship that broke on the Teeth, the reef at the harbour mouth. It rang the tides, the fog, the dead, and the return of the boats. Its note was low and cracked, and every household knew it the way they knew their own front door. Eleven nights ago, in thick fog, it stopped. The frame still stands. The rope hangs cut clean, and the bell is gone. No one heard it taken, which is the strangest part, because a bell that size cannot be moved quietly.

Since then the harbour has been uneasy in ways that are hard to name. The fishing fleet goes out only half-heartedly, because without the bell the boats have no sound to steer home by when fog closes the Teeth. The old people say the sea has been listening. The younger ones say that is nonsense, and they check the sky more often than they admit.

**The two lights.** Merrow Quay has two lighthouses, built a century apart, and they do not like each other. The **Old Light** stands on the north horn of the harbour. It is a squat, whitewashed tower with a coal-fired lamp that has been converted twice and trusted never. The **Pale Light** stands on the southern rock beyond the Teeth. It is a tall, narrow, iron-ribbed tower, cut off from land at high tide and reached by a slick causeway at low water. Each tower has its own keeper, and by a coincidence the town stopped finding funny years ago, **both keepers are called Ajax**. They are unrelated. They came to the coast from different places, by different roads, for different reasons, and they carry different histories. One arrived with a past he would like to leave unspoken. The other inherited the post and the weight of a family's quiet duty. The town tells them apart by tower and by temperament, and the keepers themselves are polite about it in the way of two people who have never quite managed to be friends. The **Net-Menders' Guild**, a loose brotherhood of fishing families, holds the old customs. Many of them believe the bell was never meant to be owned, only kept, and that its loss is a judgement for something the town has let slide. They hold their meetings in the cold loft above the smokehouse.

The **Brass & Tallow Company** is a shipping concern from the southern cities that has been buying up the quay's warehouses. It wants a deeper channel, a steam-horn fog signal and a harbour that runs on contracts rather than superstition. A modern horn would make the bell redundant, and everyone has noticed that.

The **Chapel of St. Orrin** keeps the bell's records in its vestry: the casting, the repairs, and a ledger of the names it has rung for. Its elderly sexton is reluctant to talk about the last page.

**Places worth knowing.** The *Drowned Stair* is a flight of weedy steps on the breakwater that vanishes at high tide, with a door at its foot that no one admits to using. The *Cooperage* is a shuttered workshop where the bell's rope was made. The *Lantern & Eel* is the one tavern, loud with theories. Out on the Wicker Flats stands a ruined salt-works with fresh cart tracks running to its door. Beneath the town, old smuggling tunnels connect the cellars to the shore. These tunnels are not on any map the Harbourmaster owns.

**The central mystery and its stakes.** The bell was not simply stolen. It was cast with something inside its history: the metal of the wrecked ship, and a secret about how that ship was lost and who profited. Someone wants the bell silenced, someone wants it found, and someone wants it sold. A spring storm is building out past the Teeth, and a ship is due that no one at the quay can name. If the bell is not found, or its absence is not answered, the fog-season fleet will be lost, the Company will buy the quay in all but name, and Merrow Quay will become a place that no longer hears itself.

The tone is quiet, damp and watchful: *a mystery of low voices, shared suspicion and small kindnesses*, in which every person at the quay knows a little and no one knows enough. Strangers who would never otherwise have crossed paths are drawn together by the one sound that is missing, and each of them holds a different piece of what happened on the night the harbour went silent.

The sound reaches you through the soles of your boots.

*Something beneath the cliff is keeping time.*

You climb the remaining steps. Your knees complain at the last turn, where the stairs narrow, and your shortened fingers slip once on the iron rail. In the lantern room, warm oil and scorched dust mingle with the salt air coming through the ventilation grille. Beyond the glass, Greyhaven’s roofs lie blue in the hour before dawn. The empty bell tower stands above them, its open arches holding nothing but the paling sky.

The beam passes over the bay.

A row of windows appears beyond the breakwater.

You catch the railing. There are six windows, low and square, with washing strung between the middle pair. A pale sleeve lifts without wind. Behind the nearest pane stands a figure with one shoulder higher than the other—the posture your mentor carried for the last twelve years of his life.

Then the lantern turns, and there is only water.

You keep your hand on the railing until the pressure hurts.

From the stairwell comes a voice. “Ajax?”

Lio Fen stands three steps below the landing, holding a coil of rope against his patched sweater. His white streak of hair is bright in the lamplight; the rest is plastered to his forehead. He has climbed quickly, but he does not seem out of breath. He seems to be listening for someone climbing after him.

“The door was on the latch,” he says. “I knocked. You didn’t answer.”

“You’re wet.”

“Only from the knees down.” He looks at his trousers as though this distinction matters. “I didn’t go into the water.”

You open the cupboard beneath the workbench and give him a towel. He accepts it with the hand not carrying the rope, wipes his face once, then forgets it.

“I heard it again,” he says.

“The bell?”

He nods. “Under the fish-market stairs. I thought if I came here, you could tell me whether your light had seen anything.”

The mechanism ticks. You turn toward the glass before he does.

This time, the windows are dark. Beneath them a strip of paving shows through the black water, its pale joints forming a straight line toward shore. You know the harbour bottom there. You have watched boats ground on its mud for thirty years.

“There,” Lio whispers.

His rope shifts against his chest.

You look down. A loop has tightened around his wrist, though his fingers are open. He notices your gaze and pulls at it with a small, frightened impatience.

“Put it on the bench.”

“I’m trying.”

You support the coil while he works his wrist free. The rope is cold enough to ache against your palms. Its knots are intricate, folded inward around empty spaces, and from somewhere inside one of those spaces comes a thin trickle of water.

The beam reaches the shoreward end of the paving. For a moment, you see a narrow door set into the cliff below the lighthouse, where no door belongs. Water touches its threshold. Above it, cut into the lintel, is the same branching mark stamped on the oldest volume in your archive.

Lio grips the towel in both hands. “Please,” he says. “Before you go down there, tell me what that mark means.”
